About the method

Who was William Bengston?

Dr. William (Bill) Bengston (1950–2025) was an American sociologist. For 40 years he taught statistics and research methods at St. Joseph’s College (now St. Joseph’s University) in New York, and from 2010 to 2022 he was president of the Society for Scientific Exploration.

He spent more than four decades studying hands-on healing, and described himself as a skeptic who set out to test it rather than a believer. He died at home on April 16, 2025, aged 75.

How it started

In the summer of 1971, 21-year-old Bengston was working as a lifeguard on Long Island when he met Bennett Mayrick, a middle-aged house cleaner said to have psychic and healing abilities.

Over several months of questions and self-observation, the two worked out a technique of very rapid mental imaging combined with hands-on practice. Bengston called the mental part Image Cycling®.

The mouse experiments

To test the healing under controlled conditions, Bengston set up an experiment at Queens College, City University of New York, with the geologist David Krinsley. Mayrick declined to take part at the last minute, so Bengston did the healing himself.

The mice had been injected with a mammary cancer that is normally 100% fatal within about 27 days. According to the paper, all five treated mice were cured, and they then resisted the same cancer when injected again. Bengston and Krinsley published the results in the Journal of Scientific Exploration in 2000.

Later rounds used skeptical volunteers trained in the method and reported a combined cure rate of 87.9% in 33 mice. Oddly, untreated control mice often recovered too. That led Bengston to propose resonant bonding: the idea that the healer, the treated animals and the controls become connected.

How Image Cycling works
  1. Write a list of at least 20 specific things you want for yourself. Make them concrete: not “be happy”, but a specific place you want to visit.
  2. Turn each item into one vivid image that shows it already achieved, as if it’s happening now.
  3. Memorise the list in order, so each image comes to mind instantly, like flipping a flip-book.
  4. Cycle through the images in your mind: about one per second at first, then two per second, then faster, until the whole list flashes by in a second or so. As you speed up, let go of the feelings attached to each image.
  5. Keep it light and relaxed, and practise a little every day. When you get something on your list, or stop wanting it, replace it with something new.

Advanced students go on to hypercycling: placing all the images on an imagined spinning object, like a rolodex or a clock face. In the healing method, the healer cycles while resting their hands on the person, without focusing on the outcome.

Why cycle fast?

The images don’t do the healing. Their job is to keep your thinking mind busy so it stays out of the way. Bennett Mayrick noticed he healed better while chatting about something unrelated than when he concentrated, and Image Cycling was built to recreate that on purpose.

Bengston compared the conscious mind to a guard dog: throw it a steak to keep it busy while the real work happens. The details follow from that:

  • A selfish list: the images are things you want, not things for someone else. Feeding the ego what it secretly wants keeps it content and quiet.
  • Speed: going fast means you can’t dwell on, judge or hope about any one image. That’s why you let go of the feelings as you speed up.
  • No focus on the outcome: trying to will a result is exactly the interference the method avoids. Stay detached, even a little bored.
  • No belief needed: the method doesn’t depend on faith, which is why skeptical volunteers could use it in the mouse studies.

Why the healing itself would work, Bengston openly said he didn’t know; his best guess was resonant bonding. The mental side is easier to recognise: rapid cycling is a lot like focused-attention meditation. It gives the mind one simple task, interrupts worry and overthinking, and tends to bring a relaxed, absorbed state.

Getting what’s on your list

Bengston developed Image Cycling for healing, but people who practised it reported a side effect: things on their lists tended to come true, without them trying to make it happen. That is why Image Cycling spread online as a manifestation technique.

Bengston himself didn’t believe in “manifesting” in the sense of creating something from nothing. Instead, he speculated about a “teleological pull from the future” drawing the outcomes on your list toward you. This app follows the same approach: build your list, cycle it lightly every day, and let the results take care of themselves.

What the science says

The mouse results appeared in the Journal of Scientific Exploration and a few other journals outside mainstream medicine, and critics point out that they haven’t been widely replicated by independent labs. There are no clinical trials showing that the method treats cancer or any other illness in people.

If you cycle health goals, do it alongside medical care, never instead of it.
